VLA-Controlled Supernumerary Arm

Assistive, human-mounted supernumerary arm controlled by a vision-language-action model to carry out ADL tasks from natural-language prompts.

Timeline: 10-week summer (Jun 2 - Aug 8) Setting: Professor Slade's lab Tools: SmolVLA, LeRobot, Arduino/SO-101 Role: Prototyping, data collection, training scripts
Lab Professor Slade Assistive Robotics
Timeline Jun 2 - Aug 8 (10 weeks)
Focus
  • Vision-Language-Action control loop
  • Human-mounted SO-101 arm
  • Multi-camera sensing
  • ADL task design + evaluation

TL;DR

  • Built: A 1-DOF proof-of-concept servo arm and a human-mounted SO-101 setup for VLA-controlled ADL tasks.
  • Challenge: Poor or limited training data and early camera placements led to overfitting and weak object recognition.
  • Takeaway: Data quality and sensing placement dominated policy reliability and experiment design.

Context & Motivation

We explored how vision-language-action models can control an assistive, human-mounted supernumerary arm for activities of daily living (ADLs).

Most VLA work targets fixed-base robots in controlled environments with minimal human-robot interaction, so we focused on a wearable, human-interactive setting instead.

Example instruction

"Pick up the glasses and hand them to me."

Target tasks (examples)

  • Find and pass glasses to a user.
  • Hold a water bottle while a user drinks.
  • Stabilize a forearm during brushing teeth.

My Role

I owned day-to-day prototyping, data work, and integration tasks while co-developing the research plan with the team.

  • I built and debugged the 1-DOF proof-of-concept servo arm with Arduino wiring and basic circuitry.
  • I designed and 3D-printed mounting hardware (handles, mounting board + straps) and physical ROM stops for safety.
  • I assembled and modified the SO-101 arm for mobile use, including strap routing and camera mounts.
  • I wrote and maintained data-collection and training scripts and ran SmolVLA fine-tuning experiments.
  • I drafted trial designs and protocol notes (base tasks, variations, and scoring) for future evaluations.

Collaborators: Professor Slade, Charlie Chen, Raymond Liu

System Overview

The system follows an end-to-end observation — inference — action loop to control a wearable arm using camera inputs and natural-language prompts.

Inputs Shoulder + wrist + environment cameras, prompt, optional eye tracking
VLA inference SmolVLA policy in LeRobot
Action output Joint targets and gripper commands
Arm control SO-101 or 1-DOF servo rig
Task execution Grasp, move, and hand-off

Experiments & Results

We focused on the task "Pick up my glasses and hand them to me" while iterating camera setups and training data.

Poor quality + limited training data led to a very overfit initial model.
  • Single shoulder camera: only learned the movement pattern, lacked object recognition, failed if glasses moved, and had limited end-effector view.
  • Dual camera setup: consistently moved too far right, lacked depth perception, and failed to pick up glasses.
  • Triple camera setup: added a third environment camera for depth perception and pushed the next steps toward more data and tuning.

Failure Modes & Fixes

Only learned movement pattern; weak object recognition. Limited + poor-quality training data. Collected more data and tuned hyperparameters. Defined training improvements needed for the next model pass.

Evaluation / Protocol Draft

Protocol draft for future multi-participant trials with consistent variations and scoring.

Base tasks (4)

  • Pick up and place a utensil in an open drawer.
  • Stack plastic cups.
  • Hand me my glasses.
  • Hold up a water bottle while I drink.

Variations

  • Human movement (measured with OpenCap).
  • Workspace clutter.
  • Object orientation and identity.
  • Initial arm position.

Scoring

  • 5 trials per task-variation combination.
  • Normalized 0-1 scale with checkpoints; time-based scoring for continued interaction tasks.
  • Post-task surveys: System Usability Scale and NASA TLX.

Why this matters: it enables consistent, comparable trials once multi-participant testing begins.

Hardware setup notes

  • SO-101 looped around the base of the neck to secure to the chest.
  • iPhone shoulder mount using Continuity Camera (ultra-wide lens).
  • Wrist camera mounted on the end-effector.
  • Two lab iPhones recording the environment for OpenCap.
  • Limitation: user must keep gaze on the task due to limited spatial awareness.

Key Takeaways

  • Data quality beats model cleverness: limited training data produced an overfit model.
  • Sensing placement drives reliability: camera coverage determined whether the policy could see glasses and hand.
  • Define success metrics early: checkpoints and normalized scoring prevented ambiguous results.
  • Wearable safety is a hardware problem: ROM stops and mounting stability matter for human-mounted systems.

If I continued

  • Expand datasets across more ADL tasks and camera configurations.
  • Explore 3D/SLAM inputs from Aria-style sensors to improve depth perception.
